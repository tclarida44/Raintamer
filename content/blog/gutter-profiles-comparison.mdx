## Why the profile matters

A gutter does two jobs. The first is to catch and move water off the roof. The second is to look like it belongs on the home.

Most builders install whatever's on the truck, which usually means 5-inch K-style aluminum, the cheapest profile to make and the fastest to put up. That's fine for a typical tract home. It's the wrong call on a 1930s Tudor or a modern cantilever roofline.

Here's how to think about the three profiles we install most.

## K-Style

K-style is the most common residential profile in North America. Seen from the side, the front face has a crown-molding curve that looks decorative, but it actually does structural work: the curve gives the metal a stiffness it wouldn't have as a simple rectangle.

Two sizes are standard: 5-inch and 6-inch.

5-inch K-style holds about 1.2 gallons of water per linear foot. That's enough for most one-story homes, modest rooflines, and roofs without large continuous valleys.

6-inch K-style holds about 1.7 gallons per linear foot, roughly 40% more capacity. The 6-inch profile uses a 3-by-4 downspout instead of a 2-by-3, which moves water more than twice as fast. On steeper roofs, larger homes, or roofs with multiple valleys feeding a single gutter run, 6-inch is the right call. The cost difference is small. The performance difference is large.

K-style works on almost any traditional or transitional home. It's the right default unless you have a specific reason to pick something else.

## Box

Box profiles are exactly what they sound like: a rectangular cross-section, no decorative face. Visually, they read modern and clean. Functionally, they hold a lot of water for the depth.

Box gutters are the standard on commercial buildings. Larger profiles (6-inch and 7-inch) are designed for high-volume roofs.

On residential, box is increasingly popular on contemporary homes, modern farmhouses, and any architecture with crisp horizontal lines. They look intentional in a way K-style sometimes doesn't.

Two things to know about box gutters on residential:

1. They tend to be a small upcharge over equivalent-size K-style.
2. Because the front face is a flat plane, any waviness or alignment issue is more visible. Box gutters punish sloppy installs. They're a great profile if you've found a crew that takes the install standards seriously, and a bad choice if you haven't.

## Half Round

Half-round gutters are the historical predecessor to K-style. The cross-section is a literal half-circle.

On the right home, half-round looks fantastic. Tudor, Colonial Revival, and historic brick all read more authentically with half-round than with K-style. Most copper gutter installs are half-round profile, because half-round and copper together are the most traditional gutter detail in the United States.

Functionally, a 6-inch half-round holds slightly less water than a 6-inch K-style of the same nominal size, because the cross-section is smaller. We typically spec a half-round only when the architecture deserves it, not as a performance choice.

Half-round is mounted differently from K-style. Instead of hidden hangers behind the gutter face, half-round usually uses external brackets that are visible from the ground. This is part of the look. If you don't want to see brackets, half-round isn't for you.

## How we choose for you

When we walk a property, we're considering:

- The architecture and what looks right on the home
- The roof pitch and surface area, and how that translates to water volume
- Whether there are specific runs that take an outsized share of the roof load
- The trees on the lot and what kind of debris will end up in the gutter
- Whether copper, painted aluminum, or Galvalume is the right material
